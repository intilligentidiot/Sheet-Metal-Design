# Why Your Complex Sheet Metal Part Costs Far More Than It Should (And How Better DFM Fixes It)

You have a complex sheet metal part. The CAD model looks right. The tolerances are specified. You send it out for fabrication and the quote comes back far higher than you budgeted for.

Sound familiar? You are not dealing with a pricing problem. You are dealing with a design problem disguised as one.

The cause, in most cases, is a failure to [design for manufacturability](/ DFM for short. DFM means making deliberate design decisions that reduce the cost, effort, and risk of fabricating a part, without compromising its function. It is not a checklist you run at the end of a project. It is a discipline you apply from the moment a part concept takes shape.

We provide sheet metal design services to engineering teams across the US, UK, Europe, Canada, and Australia. The industries vary. The core mistakes do not.

## When to Apply DFM and Why Earlier Is Always Better

The cost of fixing a design problem scales sharply with how late it is discovered. A DFM issue caught during concept design costs almost nothing to correct. The same issue caught after fabrication has started can cost multiples of the original part price in rework, scrap, and lost time.

- **Design stage:** Fixing a DFM problem costs almost nothing.
- **Pre-production review:** Costs time typically one revision cycle.
- **After fabrication begins:** Costs real money scrap, rework, tooling, and delay.

This is especially critical for engineers working on complex enclosures, medical storage units, or industrial equipment panels parts where the geometry is intricate, the tolerances matter, and the volume may be too low to absorb rework costs easily.

The right time to apply DFM is at the start of the design process. The second-best time is before the drawing leaves your desk.

## The Real Reasons Complex Sheet Metal Parts Get Expensive

Fabricators price based on difficulty and difficulty comes from predictable, avoidable design decisions. Here are the four most common cost drivers we see when reviewing client drawings.

### 1\. Tight Tolerances That the Process Cannot Hold Economically

Sheet metal tolerancing is fundamentally different from CNC machining. In machining, ±0.002" is routine. In sheet metal, a single bend typically carries a tolerance of ±0.010", and that stacks with every additional bend in the part. Specifying machining-level tolerances on a formed sheet metal enclosure forces the fabricator into slow, manual operations and they price accordingly.

The result: you pay a significant premium for tolerances that, in most cases, are not functionally necessary.

### 2\. Bend Radii That Force Specialty Tooling

Default bend radius values in SolidWorks and similar CAD packages do not match what most fabricators run on their press brakes. A design that assumes a tight bend radius the shop cannot achieve with standard tooling triggers one of two outcomes: a custom tool order adding weeks of lead time and significant cost or a return to the engineer for redesign, adding delay without adding value.

### 3\. Features That Look Simple in CAD but Are Expensive to Form

Embossments, louvers, complex cutouts near bend lines, and flanges too short to seat properly in a V-die are common examples. Each creates friction in the quoting and production process. Fabricators either add contingency to the quote or flag the design for redesign. Either way, it costs you time and money.

### 4\. Incomplete or Ambiguous Shop Drawings

Missing bend directions, unclear hole callouts, absent finish specifications, and dimensions that conflict between the 3D model and the 2D drawing are among the most common causes of rework and cost escalation. A fabricator who cannot quote confidently from your drawings will either add buffer to the price or come back with clarification requests sometimes multiple times before production can begin.

## What DFM-First Sheet Metal Design Looks Like in Practice

Each of the four cost drivers above has a corresponding fix. Here is what applying DFM correctly looks like for each one.

### Fix 1: Tolerance Only What Is Functionally Critical

Walk through every dimension on the part and ask: what happens if this is off by 0.015"? If the answer is nothing important, the tolerance does not need to be tight. Reserve close tolerances for mating surfaces, mounting holes, and functional interfaces. Everywhere else, give the fabricator room to work efficiently.

This single change tolerancing only what matters is consistently one of the fastest ways to reduce fabrication cost without changing the part in any meaningful way.

### Fix 2: Design to the Fabricator's Tooling, Not to CAD Defaults

The best sheet metal designs are built with a specific fabrication process in mind. That means knowing which press brake tooling is available, what the shop's standard bend radius values are, and whether the part will be laser cut or CNC punched. When we handles a complex enclosure project, this dialogue happens before the model is finalised not after the quote comes back too high.

Aligning bend radii to the fabricator's standard tooling library is a small design change that removes a large cost premium.

### Fix 3: Eliminate Features That Add Cost Without Adding Function

Complex features are sometimes necessary. Often, they are not. A structured DFM review will identify louvers, embossments, or cutout geometries that can be simplified or replaced without any loss of function and with a significant reduction in fabrication cost and lead time.

The question to ask for every non-standard feature is: does this earn its cost? If the answer is unclear, it usually does not.

### Fix 4: Produce Shop Drawings That Fabricators Can Actually Use

A complete sheet metal shop drawing communicates everything the fabricator needs without a single follow-up question. That means all bend angles and directions clearly indicated, tolerances explicitly called out on critical dimensions, finish and material specifications complete, and hardware callouts matched to standard fastener libraries.

We produces sheet metal shop drawings to this standard as a core part of every design engagement. The goal is a drawing package that goes straight to production no clarification loops, no revision cycles driven by documentation gaps.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

A client came to us with a 14-bend sheet metal enclosure for an industrial control unit. The original design had been quoted by two fabricators, both returning prices significantly above budget. The drawings specified machining-level tolerances across all dimensions, a bend radius that required a custom punch, and a louver pattern positioned too close to a bend line.

After a [DFM review](/ we made three changes: tolerances were relaxed on non-critical dimensions, the bend radius was adjusted to match the fabricator's standard tooling, and the louver pattern was repositioned. The part geometry and function did not change. The fabrication cost dropped substantially and the part went into production within the original timeline.

This is not an unusual outcome. It is what DFM applied early consistently produces.

## DFM Checklist: Before Your Sheet Metal Drawing Leaves Your Desk

Use this before sending any complex sheet metal part for fabrication:

- Are bend radii matched to the fabricator's standard tooling not CAD software defaults?
- Are tolerances tight only where they are functionally required?
- Is every flange long enough to seat properly in the V-die (minimum 3× material thickness)?
- Are holes placed at least one material thickness away from any bend line?
- Are all bend angles and directions explicitly indicated on the shop drawing?
- Are hardware hole sizes matched to standard fastener libraries (e.g., PEM)?
- Are finish specifications current and achievable by modern fabrication shops?
- Does the flat pattern unfold accurately in CAD using the correct K-factor for the material and tooling not the software default?
- Are all dimensions consistent between the 3D model and the 2D shop drawing?
- Are features near bend lines (louvers, cutouts, embossments) positioned to avoid tooling interference?
